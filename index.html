What is Monte Carlo, and what are we assuming?

The one idea

When a math problem is too hard to solve exactly, simulate it with lots of random numbers and let the average give you the answer.

Picture it

You want to know what fraction of a jar of jellybeans is red. Counting every bean is the "exact" answer. Grabbing 500 random beans and counting the red ones is the Monte Carlo answer. It is not perfect, but it gets closer the bigger your handful.

Why "Monte Carlo"?

Named after the casino in Monaco. Casinos run on randomness, and so does this method.

Three things Monte Carlo is used for

Optimization

Finding the best value of something by trying random guesses.

Numerical integration

Finding areas under curves (Module 2).

Generating samples

Producing random numbers that follow a given distribution.

Use it when the closed-form answer (a clean formula you can solve on paper) is hard or impossible.

Pseudorandom numbers, in four bullets

  1. Computers cannot make truly random numbers. They run a hidden recipe (algorithm).
  2. The recipe starts from a number called the seed. Same seed → exact same "random" numbers every time. So they are pseudo (fake) random, also called deterministic.
  3. They are still statistically random: random enough for statistics. We just call them "random" from here on.
  4. Setting the seed lets other people reproduce your results. In R: set.seed(9999).

The one assumption: we can draw from Unif(0, 1)

X ~ Unif(0, 1)
"X is a random number between 0 and 1, and every value is equally likely."
f(x) = 1 if x ∈ (0,1), 0 otherwise
The density is a flat line at height 1 between 0 and 1, and zero everywhere else. Flat = equally likely.
runif(n)
R command: give me n uniform random numbers between 0 and 1. runif(n, a, b) gives them between a and b.

R's generator is called the Mersenne Twister. You only need to know the name.

Check yourself

Why do we call set.seed() before a simulation?

So the random numbers come out the same every run. That makes the result reproducible: you (or your grader) get the same answer each time.

Are computer random numbers truly random?

No. They are pseudorandom: produced by a deterministic algorithm from a seed. But they behave randomly enough for statistical work.

When is Monte Carlo the right tool?

When the exact (closed-form) answer is difficult or impossible to compute.

Example 1: Finding an area by throwing darts

The one idea

Area of the weird shape = area of the box × fraction of random darts that land in the shape.

Picture it

A puddle sits on a 10 m × 10 m patio (100 m²). It starts raining evenly. If 30% of raindrops land in the puddle, the puddle is about 30% of the patio: 30 m². You never measured the puddle's edge.

The recipe

  1. Draw a rectangle A that fully contains the shape D. You can compute the rectangle's area easily: width × height.
  2. Throw n random points uniformly inside A (say n = 1000).
  3. Count M = how many landed inside D.
  4. Estimate: area of D ≈ area of A × M / n.

The math, translated line by line

D ⊂ ℝ²
D is a shape on a flat 2-D plane. (ℝ² = the x-y plane.)
A : [x₁,x₂] × [y₁,y₂] ⊃ D
A is the rectangle from x₁ to x₂ across and y₁ to y₂ up, and it contains D (⊃ means "contains").
S(A) = (x₂−x₁)(y₂−y₁)
S( ) means "area of". Rectangle area = width × height.
p = S(D) / S(A)
p is the chance one random dart lands in D. It equals the fraction of the box that D covers. This is the unknown we want.
M ~ Bin(n, p)
Counting "hits" out of n independent tries, each with success chance p, is exactly a Binomial count.
E(M) = np, Var(M) = np(1−p)
Standard Binomial facts: average hits = n·p; spread = n·p·(1−p). Memorize these two.
p̂ = M / n
The hat ^ means "estimate of". Our estimate of p is just the fraction of hits.
Ŝ(D) = S(A) · M / n
Our area estimate = box area × hit fraction.

Is it a good estimator? Two checks.

Check 1 · Unbiased

On average, it hits the true answer.

E[Ŝ(D)] = S(A)/n · E(M)
= S(A)/n · np
= S(A) · p
= S(A) · S(D)/S(A) = S(D) ✓

Move the constant S(A)/n outside E[ ], plug in E(M) = np, the n's cancel, then p = S(D)/S(A) and S(A) cancels.

Check 2 · Consistent

With more darts, the wobble shrinks to zero.

Var[Ŝ(D)] = (S(A)/n)² · Var(M)
= (S(A)/n)² · np(1−p)
= S(A)² p(1−p) / n
→ 0 as n → ∞ ✓

Constants come out of Var squared. One n cancels, leaving n on the bottom. Bigger n → smaller variance.

Ŝ(D) →P S(D)
Converges in probability (= consistent): the chance of being far off goes to zero as n grows.
Ŝ(D) →a.s. S(D)
Converges almost surely: a stronger guarantee. The estimate itself settles on the truth. Comes from the Strong Law of Large Numbers.

Example 1a: Estimating π with this exact trick

  1. Shape D = circle of radius 1 centered at (0,0): all points with x² + y² < 1. Its area is πr² = π·1² = π.
  2. Box A = the square from −1 to 1 in both directions. Area = 2 × 2 = 4.
  3. Throw n points in the square. A point is inside the circle if x² + y² < 1.
  4. π̂ = 4 × M / n.

The R code, line by line

# Same seed → same "random" numbers
set.seed(9999)
n <- 1000                # how many darts
X <- runif(n, -1, 1)     # n random x-coordinates in [-1, 1]
Y <- runif(n, -1, 1)     # n random y-coordinates in [-1, 1]
R2 <- X^2 + Y^2          # squared distance from center, for each dart
M <- sum(R2 < 1)         # TRUE counts as 1 → number of darts inside the circle
pihat <- (M / n) * 4     # fraction inside × box area
pihat
[1] 3.164

The plot code just draws the points with plot(X, Y) and the circle as two halves: curve(sqrt(1 - x^2)) for the top and curve(-sqrt(1 - x^2)) for the bottom.

Exam traps
  • M is Binomial(n, p), not Normal and not Uniform.
  • A constant comes out of Var squared: Var(cX) = c²Var(X). Out of E it comes out plain: E(cX) = cE(X).
  • For π, multiply by 4 (the square's area), not 2.
  • Unbiased = right on average. Consistent = gets right as n → ∞. They are different properties.
  • Bonus insight: error shrinks like 1/√n. To get 10× more accurate you need 100× more points.

Check yourself

The box has area 50. Out of 2000 points, 600 land in D. Estimate the area of D.

p̂ = 600/2000 = 0.3. Area ≈ 50 × 0.3 = 15.

Why is M a Binomial random variable?

Each point independently either lands in D (success, chance p) or not. Counting successes in n independent yes/no trials with the same p is the definition of Binomial(n, p).

Show the variance of Ŝ(D) goes to 0.

Var = S(A)²·p(1−p)/n. The top is a fixed number, the bottom n grows without limit, so the fraction → 0.

With n = 1000 the course got π̂ = 3.164. Is that a mistake?

No. It is random error from a finite sample. Larger n would land closer to 3.14159 on average.

Example 2: Monte Carlo integration

The one idea

Area under a curve = width × average height. Find the average height by checking the curve at random spots.

Picture it

A hilly field is 100 m long. You want the area of its side profile. Walk to 50 random spots, measure the hill's height at each, and average them: say 7 m. Area ≈ 100 × 7 = 700 m². That's the entire method.

The goal

I = ∫ₐᵇ g(x) dx
I = the area under the curve g(x) between x = a and x = b. g might be too messy to integrate by hand.

The trick, translated

We sneak in the Uniform(a, b) density, f(x) = 1/(b−a), by multiplying and dividing by it. Multiplying by f and by 1/f changes nothing, like multiplying by 3/3.

∫ g(x) dx = ∫ g(x) · f(x) · (1/f(x)) dx
Multiply and divide by f(x). Nothing changed yet.
= (b−a) ∫ g(x) f(x) dx
1/f(x) = b−a is a constant, so pull it out front.
= (b−a) · E[g(X)]
"∫ g(x) f(x) dx" is the definition of the expected value (average) of g(X). So: integral = width × average height.

The recipe

  1. Generate X⁽¹⁾, …, X⁽ⁿ⁾ iid from Unif(a, b). (iid = independent and identically distributed: separate draws from the same distribution. The superscript ⁽ⁱ⁾ just numbers the draws.)
  2. Plug each into g: compute g(X⁽¹⁾), …, g(X⁽ⁿ⁾). These are your "heights".
  3. Average them: Ê[g(X)] = (1/n) Σ g(X⁽ⁱ⁾). Σ means "add them all up".
  4. Multiply by the width: Îₙ = (b−a) × that average.
Why it works · The (Strong) Law of Large Numbers
(1/n) Σ X⁽ⁱ⁾ →a.s. μ
The average of many iid draws settles on the true mean μ.
P( lim |avg − μ| > ε ) = 0
Same thing, formally: the chance that the long-run average stays off by more than any small amount ε is zero.
(1/n) Σ g(X⁽ⁱ⁾) →a.s. E[g(X)]
Continuous Mapping Theorem corollary: the same holds after you plug the draws into a function g. This is what Monte Carlo integration relies on.

Example 2a: ∫₀¹ eˣ dx

True answer: e − 1 = 1.71828… Here a = 0, b = 1, so the width b − a = 1 and f(x) = 1. The integral is simply E[eX] with X ~ Unif(0,1). Estimate it by averaging eX over random X.

The R code

set.seed(123)
n <- 1000
X <- runif(n, 0, 1)   # step 1: n random spots in (0, 1)
g_X <- exp(X)         # step 2: height of e^x at each spot
mean(g_X)             # step 3: average (width is 1, so no step 4)
[1] 1.713043        # true answer 1.71828
Exam traps
  • Don't forget to multiply by (b − a) when the interval isn't (0, 1).
  • f(x) for Unif(a, b) is 1/(b−a), not 1. It equals 1 only on (0, 1).
  • The LLN is the reason it works. If asked "why does this converge?", say "Strong Law of Large Numbers".

Check yourself

Write ∫₂⁵ x² dx as an expectation.

Width = 5 − 2 = 3, X ~ Unif(2, 5). So I = 3 · E[X²].

You sample 4 values of g(X) on (0, 4): 2, 5, 3, 6. Estimate I.

Average height = (2+5+3+6)/4 = 4. Width = 4. Î = 4 × 4 = 16.

Why multiply and divide by f(x)?

To turn the integral into the form ∫ g(x) f(x) dx, which is the definition of E[g(X)]. Expectations can be estimated by averaging samples.

Notice: same recipe as Module 1, just averaging heights instead of counting hits.

Example 3: Computing expectations

The one idea

To find a distribution's mean or variance, draw lots of samples from it and compute the sample mean and sample variance.

Picture it

You want the average height of every adult in California. Measure 10,000 random people and average. The sample average stands in for the true average.

What we want, translated

E(X) = μ = ∫ x f(x) dx
The mean: the long-run average value of X. f(x) is X's density.
Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]
The variance: the average squared distance from the mean. How spread out X is.

f(x) may be known, but these integrals can be hard to compute exactly.

The recipe

  1. Generate X⁽¹⁾, …, X⁽ⁿ⁾ from f(x).
  2. Mean estimate: μ̂ = X̄ = (1/n) Σ X⁽ⁱ⁾. Just the average.
  3. Variance estimate: V̂ = (1/n) Σ (X⁽ⁱ⁾ − μ̂)². Average squared distance from your estimated mean.
(1/n) Σ g(X⁽ⁱ⁾) ≈ E[g(X)]
The master formula of this course. Average of g over samples ≈ expected value of g. Mean uses g(x) = x. Variance uses g(x) = (x − μ)².
The catch (and the rest of the course)
  • All of this needs samples from f(x). Uniform is easy. Most distributions are not.
  • "How do we sample from f(x)?" is the hard part. Later chapters cover methods for this.
  • Note: the slide's variance uses 1/n, not 1/(n−1). Use what your course uses.

Check yourself

Samples: 2, 4, 6. Estimate the mean and variance (1/n version).

μ̂ = 12/3 = 4. Squared distances: 4, 0, 4. V̂ = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.

How would you estimate E[X³]?

Cube each sample and average: (1/n) Σ (X⁽ⁱ⁾)³. That's the master formula with g(x) = x³.

What's the hard part of this approach?

Generating samples from f(x) when f isn't a simple distribution like Uniform.

Short one. Keep going while the formula is fresh.

Example 4: Bayesian inference

The one idea

In Bayesian stats the unknown θ has its own distribution. Its best guess is that distribution's average, which we estimate by sampling θ many times and averaging.

Picture it

You think a coin is probably fair (your prior). You flip it 20 times and get 15 heads (the data). Your updated belief about the coin's heads-rate is the posterior. Its average is your Bayesian estimate.

Frequentist view

θ is a fixed unknown number. Pick the θ that makes the observed data most likely.

θ̂_MLE = argmax ∏ f(yᵢ | θ)

∏ = multiply all together. argmax = "the θ that gives the biggest value". MLE = maximum likelihood estimator.

Bayesian view

θ is random. Start with a prior π(θ), update it with the data to get the posterior p(θ | y).

p(θ | y) ∝ π(θ) · ∏ f(yᵢ | θ)

∝ = "proportional to". Posterior ∝ prior × likelihood.

The pieces, translated

y₁, …, yₙ ~ f(y | θ) iid
Our data: n independent observations whose distribution depends on unknown θ. "|" reads as "given".
π(θ)
The prior: what we believe about θ before seeing data. (Here π is a name, not 3.14!)
p(θ | y) = π(θ) ∏ f(yᵢ|θ) / Z(y)
The posterior: updated belief after seeing data. Z(y) is a constant that makes it a proper distribution. Z is usually very hard to compute, which is why we write ∝ and drop it.
E(θ | y) = ∫ θ p(θ | y) dθ
The Bayesian estimator = posterior mean. Another expectation, so another job for Monte Carlo.

The recipe (same as always)

  1. Generate θ⁽¹⁾, …, θ⁽ⁿ⁾ iid from the posterior p(θ | y).
  2. Average them: θ̂_B = (1/n) Σ θ⁽ⁱ⁾.
The catch
  • Sampling from the posterior is hard, especially when Z(y) is unknown.
  • The course's answer, coming later: Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).
  • Don't confuse the prior π(θ) with the number π.

Check yourself

Posterior is proportional to what two things?

The prior π(θ) times the likelihood ∏ f(yᵢ | θ).

What is the Bayesian estimator of θ in this chapter?

The posterior mean E(θ | y), estimated by averaging samples of θ drawn from the posterior.

Frequentist vs Bayesian: what's θ in each?

Frequentist: a fixed unknown number (estimated by MLE). Bayesian: a random variable with a prior distribution.
